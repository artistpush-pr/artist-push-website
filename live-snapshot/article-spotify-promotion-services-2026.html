Type "spotify promotion" into Google and you will find hundreds of companies selling the same service: Real streams, organic growth, no bots, guaranteed results…for prices between $6 and $2,000. This isn't a copy-and-paste error, and it's not just the marketers being vague. These companies are selling completely different services — and different risks to their customers.

It doesn't matter if two companies have the exact same marketing materials and website copy; the reality is they are sourcing different products and offering them at different price points, and the outcome for the artist on Spotify for Artists is different in each case.

Just look at the lowest-priced items on their list. A $6 service is a lot cheaper than a $600 service. But it isn't the same service, scaled up or down. It's completely different. We'll explain the difference in a bit. First, some context. We do offer spotify promotion ourselves. This is why you should take this guide with a grain of salt.

However, it is also why we are well positioned to give you insight that the average review site does not have. For example, what it costs us to deliver certain services, where the fat margins are, and where your money actually goes when you purchase. And in cases where we do offer a service ourselves, we will explicitly say so.

1. Five Different Things Called "Spotify Promotion"

Bulk Engagement. Buy a quantity of engagement — 10,000 plays, 1,000 saves, 5,000 followers, X monthly listeners — and it gets delivered to you over a period of days. This is by far the cheapest service available, and it accounts for the highest volume of orders. It is also by far the most likely to be detected by Spotify's enforcement systems. More on that in section four.

Pitching. Someone sends your song to playlist curators and reports back who responded. There are self-serve options to pitch your music: SubmitHub costs roughly $0.79 to $0.98 per credit, and most submissions cost between one and three credits. So if you wanted to submit to 30–80 curators, you'd be looking at $30–$120. Groover costs roughly €2 per curator and promises a written response within seven days or refund for the contact. Then there are services that pitch your music on your behalf and charge for their time.

Paid Playlist Placement. This is different than pitching. Here, you are paying the curator directly to put your song on their playlist. You don't need to pitch to anyone. The curator accepts your payment and adds your track for a defined period of time. It is important to distinguish paid placements from pitching, because the language used to sell both services is virtually the same, and yet they provide a completely different experience to the artist. You can read our breakdown of the difference between pitching and paid placement. The short version is this: pitching is maybe — and there will be a reason why they said no. Paid placement is yes — and you paid for it.

Advertising. Spotify's ad network, or Meta/TikTok ads that drive traffic to your Spotify profile. You're paying for impressions, not outcomes. No one can give you guaranteed streams from an advertising budget, if an agency tells you otherwise they're just selling you a projection disguised as a promise.

Discovery Mode isn't really a service at all. Which is why it's on this list. Many services will happily sell you "access" to Discovery Mode. Discovery Mode is free, you access it through your Spotify for Artists account, and you pay for it in royalties: Spotify keeps 30% of the revenue of streams generated by Radio or Autoplay when your song has been added to Discovery Mode. If a service charges you to activate it, you're paying for a checkbox.

3. How the money gets divided

With curator pitching, about half should go to the curators themselves. That's the people being compensated for listening and responding. They're why you get a letter explaining why your song wasn't placed, rather than radio silence. The rest is compensation for finding the right genre-appropriate playlists, sending out the pitches, and reporting back to you. If a pitching campaign is priced at $159 and sends out 60 pitches, you might be paying approximately $1.30 for every guaranteed human listener, and the rest for the process of getting them there.

For bulk engagement, the cost to the supplier is only a small percentage of the cost you'll pay, which is why it can be marketed for $6. The markup isn't the problem — the delivery method is what you're purchasing, and it's the one detail in this industry nobody will explain.

With advertising, be careful of the split between media spend and management fees. An advertised campaign that costs $300 with $150 going to an agency is really a $150 ad campaign. Request an itemized budget before you sign off. Any service that refuses to separate the two components is hiding something.

4. The red flags to steer clear of

Guaranteed stream count. Spotify itself advises artists against third-party streaming services that promise guaranteed stream count. If a stream count is pre-guaranteed, it's not being driven organically but is being artificially pumped. Artificial pumping is what Spotify's detection mechanisms look out for.

Any promise around editorial playlists. Editorial playlists are curated and maintained by Spotify themselves. No one can sell you an editorial playlist placement. A company that claims they'll be able to get you on an editorial playlist is either bluffing or swapping it out for their own independently created playlist with an "official" sounding name.

Playlists that have lots of followers but very few listeners. A playlist with 40k followers and 200 monthly listeners is basically dead. A playlist's follower count is what's marketed to you, but its monthly listener count is the one that matters for determining if a placement is worthwhile. Don't be afraid to ask.

Lack of reporting. If a campaign finishes and all you're given is a number instead of a report with links to the placements, that's no way to determine what actually worked or not.

The question underneath all four is what happens when the activity gets flagged. On 1 April 2024 Spotify began charging labels and distributors around €10 per track when they detect flagrant artificial streaming — which Spotify defines as any track with more than 90% of its streams being artificial. Distributors then pass that cost on to the artist, often shutting down the artist's account in the process. The detection is done via partners such as Beatdapp and Pex, and Spotify will only start paying a track royalties once it has accumulated 1,000 streams. In September 2025 Spotify stated it had removed over 75 million songs from its platform as a result of spammy behavior in the preceding twelve months. If you're considering any of the cheap options on this list, weigh that risk first — it's more nuanced than simply yes or no, and it really depends on which of the five scenarios above applies to you.

5. What to expect at each level

$50 range. Self-service pitching only. With $50 you're looking at 30–50 curator submissions on a service like SubmitHub or Groover. You're responsible for writing the pitch, and choosing the playlists. Expect 1–3 placements on small playlists and a large amount of rejections, which will at least give you insight into what kind of response you'll get from your track. That's $50 well spent in music marketing and not because of the placements — it's because of the feedback.

$200 range. Managed pitching to 50–70 curators, a small-scale advertising test, or 1 placement on a really active playlist. Choose one. Don't split $200 between the three. You'll end up with three inconclusive data points. For this tier, you should get a report with links, a realistic range of several thousand streams and nothing guaranteed.

$500 and above. Here, you can do two things at once – pitching for placements and running ads for retargeting – and you can actually compare the results. This is also where you should start asking harder questions about the deliverables, since the cost should mean something other than the cost. Our own pricing page states exactly what's included in a campaign at each price bracket, including how many curators we pitch to and how long the turnaround will be, and you should demand the same information from every provider you're considering: if the landing page doesn't tell you what you'll receive and when, the offer isn't complete.

Something that no amount of money can buy: a second track release. Every campaign mentioned here is designed to promote a single track. The people who convert into fans are the ones who save the song, follow the artist and come back again of their own accord – so the most important metric to look at after any campaign is not the number of streams, but the number of saves.

Frequently asked questions

Does paid promotion negatively affect my chances of being algorithmically recommended?

Realistic engagement that looks like real listening won't. Flat, mechanical engagement will be discounted or flagged. The algorithm isn't looking at your bill; it's looking at behaviour – completion rate, saves, replays.

Is it possible to guarantee placement on Discover Weekly or Release Radar?

No, and the premise is wrong. These are feeds tailored to each listener; they are not lists that people can put songs on. You get placed in these feeds by listeners whose tastes are similar to your own.

How long does it take for a campaign to produce results?

Pitching campaigns generally take between five and fourteen days, depending on how many curators you have, and then another week or two of placements will yield measurable streams. If you see any streams within a day of ordering, you didn't order a pitching campaign.

Is it worth promoting an old track?

Maybe, and it's more affordable to experiment with than a brand-new release. Older tracks that already have a decent save rate will usually respond better to promotion than new tracks with no prior engagement history, since there's already a positive trend to build on.

What should I do before investing in a campaign?

Make sure your artist profile is fully completed, that your track has passed the 1,000-stream threshold at which Spotify starts paying royalties, and check the save rate. Promotional services simply increase whatever is already there; if your save rate is poor, you're paying to get more people to skip your song.
