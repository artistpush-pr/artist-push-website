"How do I get on Spotify playlists" is one question with three answers, and mixing them up is the reason most artists waste their first year. Editorial playlists are programmed by people who work at Spotify. Algorithmic playlists are generated per listener by software. Independent playlists belong to strangers on the internet. Three different gatekeepers, three different rules, and almost nothing you do for one of them works on the others.

Worth mentioning before anything else: we do offer playlist promotion services, so read this article with appropriate skepticism. The upside of being in our business is that we know which paths are real and which are just being sold.

Route 1: Editorial playlists

These are the ones with names you know — the genre flagships, the mood staples, New Music Friday. A team of Spotify editors decides what goes on them. There is exactly one legitimate way in: the pitch tool inside Spotify for Artists.

The rules are narrow and Spotify publishes all of them:

  • Pitch at least seven days before release. Spotify's stated reason is simply that editors need time to listen.
  • One song at a time. Once the pitched song goes live, you can pitch the next one.
  • The song must be unreleased. The moment it is live, it can no longer be pitched.
  • You cannot pitch compilations, or songs where you are only a featured artist.
  • Only someone with Admin or Editor access on the artist profile can submit.

Two things happen when you pitch, regardless of whether an editor picks the track up. First, pitching seven or more days ahead puts your song into your own followers' Release Radar on release day. That is not an editorial decision, it is automatic, and it is the single most reliable thing the pitch form does. Second, Spotify notes that songs passed over at release may still get discovered later — the pitch is not a one-shot audition that expires on Friday.

For an unsigned artist with a few thousand monthly listeners, the right editorial goal isn't the flagship playlist. It's Fresh Finds, which is actually over a dozen separate genre-focused playlists made just for independent artists like yourself. It's not giving up to aim for that. It's aiming for the playlist that was literally made to find artists where you are.

The pitch form itself is where most of the winnable margin sits — genre and mood tagging, the description, what you tell editors about the release plan. Spotify's own guidance is blunt about it: more detail gives the song a better chance. That has enough moving parts to deserve its own guide, so we will not compress it here.

Route 2: Algorithmic playlists

Nobody programmes these and nobody can place you on them. They are generated for each individual listener, which means there is no single Discover Weekly — there are hundreds of millions of them.

The ones that matter for artists:

Release Radar, refreshed Fridays, built from artists a listener follows plus adjacent recommendations. This is the one your pitch guarantees you access to.

Discover Weekly, refreshed Mondays, built on collaborative filtering — listeners whose taste overlaps with people who already engage with you.

Radio and Autoplay, which trigger off audio similarity and what happens when the track plays: does the listener sit through it or skip.

daylist, the time-and-mood playlist that refreshes through the day.

Here is the part that changed recently and that most guides have not caught up with. On 10 July 2026 Spotify rolled out session controls inside Release Radar: listeners can now narrow the playlist to a specific genre, restrict it to new-to-you artists only, or pick from up to five options including *Discover new artists* and *Editors' picks*. Discover Weekly had already gained genre selection of up to five genres.

Read that from an artist's side, because it cuts both ways. A guaranteed slot in a feed the listener can filter is no longer a guaranteed listen — a follower who narrows Release Radar to one genre will not see your release if you sit outside it. But the *Discover new artists* filter is the first mechanism Spotify has shipped that actively favours unknown artists over established ones inside a personalised feed. If you are small, some listeners are now explicitly asking to be shown you.

Discovery Mode belongs in this section too, because it is the only lever you have on algorithmic reach and plenty of services will charge you to pull it. It is free, it lives in Spotify for Artists, and you pay in royalties — Spotify keeps 30% on streams that Radio and Autoplay serve because you enrolled the track. It amplifies a signal; it does not create one.

What actually feeds all of this is engagement: saves, completion rate, repeat plays, and whether listeners go looking for you afterwards. That last part is what separates a reach spike from an audience, and turning that reach into monthly listeners is a different job from getting the reach.

One warning about this section of the internet. You will find articles quoting exact algorithmic thresholds — a 20% save rate trigger, 20,000 streams to unlock Discover Weekly, precise skip-rate cutoffs. Trace those numbers and they lead back to a promotion company's own campaign data, not to anything Spotify has published. Spotify does not disclose thresholds. Treat confident precision here as a marketing signal.

Route 3: Independent playlists

Everything not run by Spotify. Millions of lists, owned by individuals, blogs, small labels and networks, ranging from genuinely influential to completely fake.

Two ways in, and they are not the same transaction. You can pitch — submit through a platform like SubmitHub or Groover, or reach the curator directly, and get a yes, a no, or a reasoned rejection. Or you can pay for placement — the curator takes money and adds the track for a fixed window. The marketing language around both is nearly identical, which is why deciding whether to pitch a curator or pay one outright is worth doing deliberately rather than by whichever offer you clicked first.

But vetting is critical here, because it's also where the fake supply is. Three checks:

  1. Monthly listeners, not followers. A playlist with 40,000 followers and 200 monthly listeners is dead inventory. Follower count is what gets advertised.
  2. Is the playlist current? If it hasn't changed in eight months, it's not being updated. And no one's going to hear your song if it's placed on it.
  3. Does the audience look human? Playlists stuffed with 300 tracks, no coherent genre, and a name generated to catch searches are built to sell slots, not to be listened to.

We sell placements on hand-picked playlists ourselves, so apply those three checks to us as readily as to anyone else — a service confident in its inventory will hand you the listener numbers without being pushed.

What those three routes really boil down to

The same thing, which is the useful part. Editors check whether a track is already getting saved before they risk a slot on it. The algorithm reads saves and completion directly. Independent curators keep lists that perform, and a track that gets skipped hurts their playlist.

So the sequence that works is unglamorous: get a small number of real listeners engaging properly, then go asking for reach. Reach applied to a track nobody saves produces a spike and nothing after it. This is also why buying volume undermines the thing it is supposed to help — since 1 April 2024 Spotify has charged distributors around €10 per track where it detects flagrant artificial streaming, defined as more than 90% of a track's streams, and that charge lands on the artist. Detection runs through partners including Beatdapp and Pex.

The order to do it in

  1. Claim Spotify for Artists and confirm you have Admin or Editor access — without it you cannot pitch at all.
  2. Set the release date far enough out that you can pitch two to four weeks ahead rather than scraping the seven-day minimum.
  3. Pitch the track. This locks in followers' Release Radar and puts you in front of editors.
  4. In the same window, pitch independent curators. Their timelines run 5–14 days, so they overlap release week naturally.
  5. Post-release, track save rate ahead of stream count, and only turn on Discovery Mode once there's a signal worth amplifying.

FAQ

Can I pay to get on an editorial playlist?

No. Editorial lists are programmed by Spotify staff and no third party sells access. Any service offering an editorial placement is either lying or substituting an independent playlist with an official-sounding name.

What happens if I miss the seven-day pitch deadline?

You lose both the editorial consideration and the guaranteed Release Radar slot for that release, and you cannot pitch the song once it is live. The track can still be picked up algorithmically later, and your next release resets the clock.

Can I pitch a song that is already out?

No. Pitching requires an unreleased track. Some artists work around this by planning a follow-up single rather than re-pitching an old one.

Does getting on one playlist help me get on others?

Indirectly, and only if the placement performs. Engagement from a good placement is a signal the algorithm reads and editors can see. Engagement from a dead placement is not a signal at all.

How many followers do I need before any of this is worth doing?

None, for the editorial pitch — it is free and the Release Radar guarantee works at any size. For paid independent placements, wait until the track has a save rate you would be happy to amplify, because promotion multiplies whatever is already there.
