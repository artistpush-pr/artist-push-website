The metric that every artist checks but few understand is Monthly Listeners. It isn't fans. It isn't plays. It's not a total count. It's a daily recalculation of how many unique listeners streamed your music for at least thirty seconds over the last 28 days. Play three songs and you still count once. Stop playing for a month and you drop out of the number entirely.

Which means the number is a leaking bucket by design. Every day, listeners from twenty-nine days ago drop out. Growth is not adding people — it is adding them faster than the window drops them.

First, the split that matters more than the number

Spotify divides your audience in a way most artists never look at, and it reframes the whole exercise.

Monthly active listeners are people who streamed your music intentionally in the past 28 days — from your artist profile, your release pages, or their own library and playlists. Spotify breaks these into three tiers: Super Listeners at 15 or more streams in 28 days, Moderate Listeners at 3–14, and Light Listeners at 1–2.

Previously active listeners used to stream you intentionally but haven't done so in at least 28 days. They may still be hearing you through automated playlists without ever choosing you.

Programmed listeners are people who have only ever heard you through programmed sources — editorial playlists, user playlists, AI DJ, Discover Weekly, Radio, Autoplay — and have not streamed you from an active source in at least two years.

One thing to keep straight, because it trips people up: these segments describe your whole audience over the past two years, while the monthly listener figure counts only the last 28 days. The segment view is the wide picture; your monthly listener number is the slice of it that happens to be active right now. The same person can sit in your segments for months without appearing in the headline number at all.

Of those three groups, one is likely to buy a ticket, save a track or show up for the next release, and the other two are not. So the goal isn't a bigger number — it's a bigger *active* share of it. Some of the tactics below move the count, some move the share, and the ones worth doing first move both.

Tactics that boost your count and your active share

1. Optimize your profile for discovery

Discovery sends people to your profile. If your artist photo is a blurry selfie, your bio is blank and your Artist Pick is two years old, a listener who lands there out of curiosity will leave without listening to another track. This is the lowest-hanging fruit on this list, yet it's also the one most often ignored, because it feels like maintenance rather than marketing.

2. Put a Canvas on every track

A Canvas is the short looping visual behind a track in the mobile player. It costs nothing, it is per-track, and it gives a passive listener something to look at instead of a static square — which is the difference between glancing at your name and remembering it.

3. Pitch every release, at least seven days out

Pitching an unreleased track through Spotify for Artists is free, and doing it seven or more days ahead puts the song into your own followers' Release Radar on release day automatically, whether or not an editor picks it up. That is a guaranteed injection of active-source listening on day one. Missing the deadline forfeits it.

4. Find the right playlists, not the largest

A placement on an inactive playlist with 40,000 followers will do nothing for you. A placement on a tiny but engaged playlist will add listeners who act like listeners. Editorial, algorithmic and independent playlists are three completely different animals with three different sets of rules, and it's worth knowing which door you're actually trying to open before you spend anything.

5. Use Discovery Mode sparingly

Discovery Mode doesn't cost you anything up front — you only lose 30% of the royalties on any stream that comes from it via Radio and Autoplay. Discovery Mode is an amplifier, not a generator, so enrolling a track nobody finishes just wastes the 30%. Enrol your one or two strongest tracks, and know how each of the three engines behind these slots works first.

6. Build a Countdown Page for the next release

Countdown Pages let fans pre-save an upcoming album or EP, preview the tracklist and watch countdown videos. Pre-saves matter for monthly listeners specifically because they put the release into a listener's own library — and a play that starts from there counts as an active source, not a programmed one.

7. Use display campaigns when you are eligible

Marquee is the full-screen prompt on Home for a new release, delivering over roughly ten days and starting around $100. Showcase is the mobile banner, running about fourteen days and working for catalogue as well as new music. Eligibility is gated — Spotify notes that roughly one in six Discovery Mode users becomes eligible within three months — so this is a tactic that arrives after the earlier ones have done their work.

8. Release on a cadence, not in bursts

Because the window is always the last 28 days, releasing every six to eight weeks means there's almost always a brand new single sitting inside it. Releasing two singles four months apart means two spikes followed by two long falls. Releasing two singles six weeks apart means one plateau. No difference in the music, only the math.

9. Use the second track as bait

Radio and Autoplay determine what follows your song, so a listener who stays past the end is far more likely to stick around and come back than one who immediately clicks away. Organising your own catalogue — deciding what should play after your most popular song — is totally free, and almost nobody does it.

10. Focus on saves and library adds, not streams

A save is a listener volunteering to hear you again, and that's the exact kind of behaviour you want accumulating month over month inside the 28-day window. It's also what separates a spike in reach from an actual audience, so knowing what a save is worth relative to a stream matters before you optimise for the wrong thing.

11. Send outside traffic to active sources

A link from a video, a post or an email that lands on your artist profile or a release page creates an *active* listener in Spotify's classification. The same person arriving via a programmed playlist does not. Where you point the link changes which segment the listener joins.

12. Move Light listeners up a tier

You already have people at 1–2 streams per 28 days. Moving a slice of them to 3–14 is cheaper than finding strangers, and it compounds — Moderate and Super listeners stay inside the window on their own. This is what an artist profile, a Canvas, a coherent catalogue and a release rhythm are quietly for.

13. Win back the listeners you already lost

Previously active listeners are the cheapest audience in this entire list and the one nobody works. They chose you once — they streamed from their own library, your profile or a playlist they built — and then stopped inside the last 28 days. Nothing needs to be explained to them.

Two things reach them. If they followed you, a new release lands in their Release Radar automatically, which is another reason tactic 3 pays for itself. If they didn't, Showcase is the tool built for exactly this: it works on catalogue, not just new music, so you can put an old track back in front of someone who used to play it.

Watch this segment before and after a release. If previously active listeners are growing faster than monthly active ones, you are not losing reach — you are losing people, and no amount of new discovery fixes a leak that size.

Buying the number

We sell monthly listener campaigns, so treat what follows accordingly.

What a paid listener campaign moves is the reach figure. What it does not move is the segment mix — delivered listeners land as programmed listeners at best, and programmed listeners are defined by never having streamed you from an active source. The number goes up; the audience underneath it does not change shape.

That is a real use case for some artists — a label, a playlist curator or a booker looking at a profile sees the number before they see anything else, and there are situations where the number is the point. It is not a substitute for the thirteen tactics above, and anyone selling it as one is selling you something else. If you want the specifics of what a listener campaign buys and what it doesn't, they are on the service page rather than buried here.

The harder truth is that volume applied to a track nobody saves produces a bulge that leaves the window twenty-eight days later, and you are back where you started minus the money. Since 1 April 2024 Spotify has also charged distributors around €10 per track where it detects flagrant artificial streaming — more than 90% of a track's streams — and that charge reaches the artist.

What to watch

Don't open the monthly listener number first. Open the segment breakdown and look at three things: how many Super Listeners you have, whether Light listeners are growing or declining, and what percentage of your total is programmed rather than active.

An artist growing from 2,000 to 8,000 monthly listeners purely through programmed sources has a higher number and the same career. An artist growing from 2,000 to 3,000 while tripling their Super Listener tier has a lower headline number but a real audience. That second one is the one that sells tickets.

FAQ

How frequently does the monthly listener count change?

Daily. It's a rolling 28-day count, not a calendar month, so it changes every single day as new listeners come in and older ones fall out.

Why did my monthly listeners decrease when my streams didn't?

Usually it means the same listeners were playing your tracks more, replacing the unique ones. The same people playing more keeps your streams high while your unique-person count goes down. Another possibility is that you lost a playlist placement and those listeners have now aged out of the window.

Does a play count if it's under 30 seconds?

No. Plays only count if the song is played for about 30 seconds. This is why skip-heavy traffic counts toward plays but not toward listeners.

Do followers and monthly listeners impact each other?

Not directly — they're measuring different things. Followers is a cumulative number of opt-ins that never drops off; monthly listeners is a 28-day reach metric that is always dropping off. Followers do help indirectly, by getting your music into their Release Radar.

What is a good number of monthly listeners?

The question is unanswerable without the segment split. Four figures with a healthy Super Listener tier is a working audience. Six figures that are entirely programmed is a statistic.
